By reducing product volume while holding retail sticker prices steady, food producers pass on stealth price hikes that bypass casual consumer notice.

Learning how to calculate shrinkflation using standard unit price formulas allows shoppers to unmask hidden inflation and calculate the exact percentage increase per ounce.

1. The Shrinkflation Formula

To calculate the true percentage increase caused by a package downsizing, you must compare the previous unit price against the new unit price.

Old Unit Price = Old Price ÷ Old Ounces
New Unit Price = New Price ÷ New Ounces
% Increase = ((New Unit Price − Old Unit Price) ÷ Old Unit Price) × 100

2. Practical Example: The 18 oz to 15 oz Cereal Downsize

Suppose a cereal box previously held 18 ounces and cost $4.00 ($0.222 per ounce). The manufacturer downsizes the box to 15 ounces while keeping the price at $4.00:

  • Old Unit Price: $4.00 ÷ 18 oz = $0.222 / oz
  • New Unit Price: $4.00 ÷ 15 oz = $0.266 / oz
  • Effective Inflation: (($0.266 − $0.222) ÷ $0.222) × 100 = 19.8% Price Hike

Even though the sticker price stayed at $4.00, you are paying nearly 20% more per ounce. You can test multiple package dimensions instantly using our free Grocery Unit Price Calculator.

Shrinkflation FAQ

What is shrinkflation?

Shrinkflation is when a manufacturer reduces the size or quantity of a product while keeping the price the same (or raising it), which raises the real price per ounce, per count, or per sheet.

How do you calculate shrinkflation?

Divide the old price by the old size, then the new price by the new size. The percentage increase is the new unit price minus the old unit price, divided by the old unit price, times 100. In the cereal example above, that is a 19.8% hike.

How can I tell if a product has shrunk?

Compare the net weight on the package to your last purchase or your price-book entry, and check the unit price on the shelf tag. Shelf tags are sometimes not updated right away, so verify the size yourself.

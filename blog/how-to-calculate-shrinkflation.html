How to Calculate Shrinkflation: The Math Behind Smaller Packaging and Higher Unit Costs

By Savings Tactic Editorial Team • Published October 26, 2026

When a box of cereal drops from 18 ounces to 15.3 ounces while keeping the exact same $4.99 shelf price, the manufacturer hasn't lowered the price—they have implemented shrinkflation. By reducing product volume while holding retail sticker prices steady, food producers pass on stealth price hikes that bypass casual consumer notice.

Learning how to calculate shrinkflation using standard unit price formulas allows shoppers to unmask hidden inflation and calculate the exact percentage increase per ounce.

1. The Shrinkflation Formula

To calculate the true percentage increase caused by a package downsizing, you must compare the previous unit price against the new unit price.

Old Unit Price = Old Price ÷ Old Ounces
New Unit Price = New Price ÷ New Ounces
% Increase = ((New Unit Price − Old Unit Price) ÷ Old Unit Price) × 100

2. Practical Example: The 18 oz to 15 oz Cereal Downsize

Suppose a cereal box previously held 18 ounces and cost $4.00 ($0.222 per ounce). The manufacturer downsizes the box to 15 ounces while keeping the price at $4.00:

  • Old Unit Price: $4.00 ÷ 18 oz = $0.222 / oz
  • New Unit Price: $4.00 ÷ 15 oz = $0.266 / oz
  • Effective Inflation: (($0.266 − $0.222) ÷ $0.222) × 100 = 19.8% Price Hike
